Tales From Below

Hell's Tales is built as an anthology: separate stories, eras, characters, and sins connected by one question—what if the version of evil we were taught was written by the side that won the argument? Each tale can stand alone while contributing to a larger mythology of Hell, Heaven, Satan, organized religion, and human hypocrisy.

The central idea

Who benefits when evil is simple?

Hell's Tales examines the gap between professed morality and practiced morality. Organized religion can promise compassion, humility, forgiveness, and charity while institutions and leaders sometimes pursue status, wealth, social control, exclusion, or political power. The stories live inside that contradiction.

The anthology also treats Satan less as a fixed cartoon villain and more as a contested character: adversary, dissenter, scapegoat, witness, tempter, prosecutor, rebel, or even inconvenient truth-teller depending on the story. Hell's Tales does not require the reader to accept one theological answer. It asks the reader to inspect who is speaking, who is judging, and who gets silenced.

In this universe, damnation is rarely as simple as horns versus halos. Human institutions can be cruel. Sinners can be honest. Saints can be performative. Demons can tell the truth. And the most dangerous evil may be the kind that arrives wearing the language of virtue.

The Accused

Satan is the gravitational center of the anthology, but not always its protagonist. Different stories can contradict one another, allowing readers to encounter competing versions of the Devil just as theology, folklore, literature, and culture have done for centuries.

The inherited story

The Devil as absolute evil

The traditional accusation is familiar: Satan deceives, tempts, corrupts, rebels, and drags humanity away from salvation. Hell's Tales uses that image because its cultural power is enormous—but it refuses to leave the image unexamined.

A villain is easiest to understand when only his enemies are allowed to write the biography.

The forbidden possibility

What if the adversary has a case?

Some stories may present Satan as a manipulator. Others may present him as a rebel against authoritarian power, a custodian of unwanted truths, a scapegoat for human cruelty, or a being whose crimes have been enlarged by centuries of fear. The point is not to replace one dogma with another, but to make certainty uncomfortable.

Hell becomes more interesting when the Devil is allowed to testify.

Tale I

The Bargain

✦ ⛧ ✦

Elias came to Hell expecting sulfur, screaming, and the laughter of a monster. Instead, he found a quiet office, a wooden desk, and a man in a dark suit reading every promise Elias had ever made.

“You called me a liar your entire life,” the stranger said without looking up. “So I thought we might begin with yours.”

Behind him, shelves disappeared into darkness. Each held ledgers filled not with sins, but with contracts—marriage vows, oaths of office, pledges from pulpits, and promises whispered beside hospital beds.

☿

The Devil pushed one ledger across the desk. Elias recognized his own handwriting on the first page, though he had never seen the book before.

“I do not need souls,” Satan told him. “Human beings give pieces of themselves away every day. To churches. To nations. To employers. To fear. You simply noticed mine because someone taught you to be frightened of the signature.”

For the first time since dying, Elias wondered whether damnation had begun long before he reached Hell.

Tale II

The Pious

♱ ✦ ♱

Reverend Carrow arrived at the gate smiling. He had spent forty-three years promising his congregation that men like him would be welcomed by trumpets.

No trumpets sounded. A line of people waited instead: a frightened teenager he had publicly shamed, a widow he had convinced to donate rent money, a family he had cast out to protect the church's reputation.

Carrow raised his Bible like a legal document. The gatekeeper laughed. “We have a copy,” she said. “We also have the receipts.”

3
Tale II · continued

The Pious

⚖

The trial contained no questions about belief. No one asked whether Carrow had been saved, baptized, ordained, or forgiven.

They asked whom he had harmed. They asked what he knew when he harmed them. They asked how often he had mistaken authority for virtue and applause for evidence of God.

When the sentence came, Satan was not present. Carrow demanded to know why. “This one,” said the judge, “was entirely yours.”

Tale III

The Accuser

⛧ ✦ ⛧

Heaven convened the hearing after humanity's final war. The charge was ancient: corruption of mankind.

Satan stood alone beneath a ceiling made of light. “Produce the evidence,” he said.

The prosecution brought empires, crusades, inquisitions, prisons, markets, cults, tyrants, and massacres. Satan listened patiently. Then he asked the same question after every exhibit: “Where was I?”

5
Tale III · continued

The Accuser

☠

The court fell silent when the defense called its first witness: humanity itself.

Men and women admitted they had invoked the Devil to explain appetites they already possessed. Greed needed a tempter. Violence needed a whisper. Hatred needed a supernatural author.

Satan looked toward the throne. “You gave them free will,” he said. “And then you gave them me, so they would never have to admit what they did with it.”

About the anthology

Hell is the setting. Judgment is the subject.

Hell's Tales is a dark anthology centered on Hell and the Devil. Its stories explore faith, religious authority, guilt, temptation, punishment, moral hypocrisy, institutional power, rebellion, and the human need to divide the world into the saved and the damned.

The anthology uses horror, dark fantasy, satire, theological inversion, and psychological storytelling to challenge familiar assumptions. It is deliberately skeptical of easy moral categories. A priest can be monstrous. A demon can be compassionate. A believer can be faithless. A condemned soul can possess more integrity than the institution that condemned them.

The purpose is not to provide a new creed. It is to tell unsettling stories that force the reader to ask whether evil is defined by conduct, authority, reputation, scripture, power—or something much harder to manipulate.
