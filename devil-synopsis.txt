The Devil in Hell's Tales
Universe synopsis and writing notes

Satan keeps the records Heaven discarded. Human beings invoke his name to explain decisions he often had no part in, and he has spent centuries returning those decisions to their authors. He has crimes of his own to answer for. His testimony matters because he remembers the people who made the laws now used to condemn him.

Before the throne

The oldest infernal account begins with a claim: before there was God, there was YHWH. Heaven was governed by Elders whose decisions could be challenged. Councils argued over the ancient laws. Lucifer belonged to that order and loved YHWH as a brother.

When the councils divided into factions and stopped governing, Lucifer proposed that YHWH take temporary command. He argued publicly for his friend and helped win the vote. YHWH settled disputes and restored neglected duties. Lucifer believed the appointment had saved Heaven.

YHWH then asked for messengers, guards and scribes. Judges required more authority to administer his decrees. Each request followed from powers he had already been given. When Lucifer asked him to restore the Elders, YHWH refused. Lucifer brought the matter before the Assembly. The angels who had supported him would no longer speak.

YHWH sent Lucifer to retrieve Elders who had withdrawn during the crisis, claiming this would restore the old government. While Lucifer was away, the councils were dissolved and the archives seized. The guardians swore new oaths. Lucifer returned to find his friend seated on a throne, with the Elders standing beneath it.

Lucifer knelt. He decided then to overthrow YHWH.

The failed rebellion

The rebels recruited through private conversations. Some wanted the old councils restored; others feared or hated the new ruler. Lucifer believed he owed Heaven a debt for helping YHWH gain power.

A general betrayed the plan. YHWH knew when the rebels would move and where they would enter. They were surrounded before most understood they had been discovered. Lucifer ordered surrender to spare the remaining angels. Heaven recorded the decision as cowardice.

The prisoners were tried for blasphemy. Lucifer's conversations with his former friend were entered as evidence of conspiracy. YHWH attended the sentencing and watched him throughout.

Hell had not yet been founded. The rebels were expelled into unfinished creation. They wandered for eons while storms and burning stars drove them from each refuge. Lucifer believed YHWH was pursuing them, making sure that every possible sanctuary knew their crimes.

They eventually reached a dead realm of ash and burning mountains. Its caverns were deep enough to return an echo hours later. There they discovered that YHWH could no longer see them. Lucifer persuaded the others to stay. They built shelters, roads and fortifications. The realm became Hell.

Hell and its ruler

Hell has courts and archives, but also markets, feasts and residents who quarrel about its government. Fallen angels disagree over whether the rebellion justified what it cost them. Some respect Satan; others tolerate him because he opposes Heaven. His authority is open to resentment and ridicule.

He keeps records partly to resist Heaven's account of the Fall. He remembers choosing YHWH and remains responsible for that choice. His opposition to God is inseparable from his memory of loving the friend who took the throne.

That history does not excuse Satan's conduct. He lies and manipulates. He makes bargains with desperate people and sometimes treats their suffering as useful evidence against Heaven. His contempt for hypocrisy can turn into contempt for ordinary weakness. He may expose a lie without caring whether the person who depended on it survives the exposure.

His own certainty is dangerous. He has accused YHWH for so long that he can forget to question his account. Hell can become corrupt under him, and opposing Heaven gives it no protection from that risk.

Human responsibility

The human witnesses often blame Satan for choices they made themselves. A ruler ordered the attack. A merchant knew the granaries were full. A clergyman protected an abuser. Satan's questions return the testimony to the moment each person could have acted differently.

His bargains usually concern a desire the other party already has. He asks what they will pay for it. The terms can be explicit even when his motives are concealed. A person may accept because the offer resembles arrangements they have been making all their life.

In The Accuser, he contests humanity's attempt to assign him its crimes. In other stories he may tempt a soul, examine a record or recount his exile. These accounts can disagree. His memory and pride deserve scrutiny alongside the official histories.

Heaven's claim

Heaven condemns souls under its own rules of allegiance. Hell receives them and examines their conduct before determining punishment. The distinction allows a person to have been cruel for reasons Heaven ignored, or compassionate in ways Heaven punished.

Satan uses that discrepancy against the throne. He asks whether praise extracted under threat can be love and whether an eternal sentence can correct a finite act. Heaven benefits when all evil is assigned to its adversary. Its servants can then invoke him when asked to account for their own harm.

Satan must face the same scrutiny. The people injured by his bargains remain people, even when their cases help his argument. He can be right about Heaven and still owe them an answer.

The public calendar

The Fall commemorates the exile and gives its veterans another occasion to dispute the rebellion. On the Day of Questions, residents can challenge Satan and the claims of either realm without punishment. The Day of the Accused requires accusers to defend themselves before the people they condemned.

Satan hosts the Feast of the Scapegoat for those blamed to protect someone more powerful. On Judgment Night he reads complaints about Hell aloud. These observances allow criticism of the realm and its ruler. They cannot establish that either deserves trust; the conduct of the courts must do that.

Appearance and speech

Satan should look old enough to have lived through the histories he disputes. Formal clothes suit his appearances in courts and offices. A grotesque or horned form can be used when a story examines the image others have assigned him.

He generally speaks with restraint. A precise question can force a witness to name what they did. Humor can expose an excuse, though his pleasure in humiliating someone may itself become cruel. When he loses control, the departure from his usual restraint should have a reason in the scene.

Recurring roles

The Accused answers charges brought by Heaven and asks for evidence.
The Adversary challenges a ruler who claims unquestioned authority.
The Tempter asks what a person is willing to trade.
The Archivist keeps suppressed testimony and earlier versions of official records.
The Scapegoat receives blame for other people's decisions.
The First Exile remembers Heaven before the throne and Hell before its settlement.
The Prosecutor questions those who once condemned others.
The Unreliable Witness tells an account shaped by pride and old grief.

The Infernal Code

Satan resents stupidity as a refusal to think. Hypocrisy recalls the claims Heaven made during his trial. Conformity and willful ignorance remind him of angels who watched YHWH consolidate power. Cowardice includes his former allies' silence before the Assembly. Certainty troubles him because it can excuse abuses committed in Hell as easily as in Heaven.

The six Infernal Acts also implicate him. He condemns betrayal while others name him traitor. His contracts can exploit desperation. He sometimes reduces a witness to an argument and practices the cruelty he recognizes in others. Corruption exists within the realm he governs. His judgment of YHWH may leave little room for the understanding his own courts demand.

The longer story

Lucifer wants to return to Heaven and correct the choice that helped enthrone YHWH. Conquest alone would not resolve it. He wants those inside to remember when the councils could disagree and no single angel's word ended a question. His return would test whether Heaven could hear him without ordering another exile.

Writing notes

Keep the cost of Satan's charm visible. Give people harmed by him room to speak. His past love for YHWH should complicate their encounters, and his guilt over the appointment should affect what he is prepared to do next. Let his claims be checked against other witnesses. Neither realm's name establishes its honesty.
