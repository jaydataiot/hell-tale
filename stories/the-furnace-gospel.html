Tales From Below · Tale V

The Furnace Gospel

A book survives nine hundred years of fire and opens with a sentence no saved man wanted copied.

Approx. Time
September 9, ash-hour at the Scriptorium of Smoke.
Holiday
The Festival of Forbidden Knowledge.
Reader Note
The manuscript is opened while Hell publicly reads censored histories and truths Heaven preferred buried.
First Descent

The book was found in a furnace that had burned for nine hundred years. Its pages were hot but unconsumed. Its binding had no title, only the impression of two hands pulling in opposite directions. The monks who discovered it carried it to the scriptorium with iron tongs and argued for three days over who had permission to read it.

On the fourth day, the book opened by itself. Brother Caldus was chosen because he had copied more approved terrors than any living man. His devils had claws. His sinners begged.

His flames curled beautifully around the margins. The first line offended him. Hell was not made for pain. Hell was made for truth.

“Heretical,” said Abbot Severin, without reading further. The book turned a page. Every lie creates the fire required to reveal it. The abbey had been built over the Furnace Gospel’s chamber.

Its monks copied warnings for kings, indulgences for merchants, and histories in which every victorious patron had been favored by God. They called the furnace sacred because it destroyed drafts that contradicted the final version. Caldus had fed it many pages. That night he returned alone.

The book waited on the lectern. Beside it lay a pen cut from a black feather. “What do you want copied?” he asked. The pages filled with his own handwriting.

Brother Caldus had entered the abbey at eleven, delivered there by an uncle who considered curiosity a defect best corrected by discipline. The scriptorium taught him beauty before it taught him truth. He learned to grind lapis into blue, gold leaf into halos, and human uncertainty into clean margins. The first manuscript he copied contained three contradictory accounts of a saint's death. When he asked which was true, Severin answered, “The one that best teaches obedience.” Caldus remembered the sentence only when the Furnace Gospel began returning everything he had helped erase.

He saw the Chronicle of Saint Vey, commissioned by Duke Orlan after the duke slaughtered a village. Caldus had changed slaughtered to pacified. He had changed children to insurgents. In the margin, he had painted an angel offering Orlan a crown.

“I copied what the abbot gave me.” The sentence appeared beneath his defense: A hand does not become innocent because another mouth commands it. Caldus closed the book. Heat pulsed through the cover. The next morning, Severin ordered it destroyed.

They chained the volume, carried it to the furnace, and threw it into the white center. For a moment the book disappeared. Then every manuscript in the abbey began to smoke. Words rose from parchment in black ribbons.

Saints lost borrowed miracles. Kings recovered their massacres. Martyrs admitted fear. Heretics regained the sentences removed from their testimony.

The arrival of pilgrims transformed the abbey overnight. Vendors built stalls along the road. Nobles sent agents demanding private readings. Sick people pressed burned cloth to their bodies. A mother carried a feverish child three days through rain because someone told her the letters in the wall could heal. Severin saw danger and opportunity in equal measure. He established an official queue, an official donation, and an official interpretation. Caldus watched a mystery become an institution in less than a week. The Gospel responded by writing across the sealed stones: A gatekeeper is often a frightened pilgrim who discovered he enjoys the key.

The monks ran through the library beating at truth with wet cloths. The Gospel lay on the furnace floor, open and unharmed. “Seal the chamber,” Severin commanded. They built a wall before sunset.

At midnight, letters burned through the stones. Caldus read them from his cell: CERTAINTY IS THE LAST IDOL BECAUSE IT TEACHES THE WORSHIPPER TO CALL HIS OWN VOICE DIVINE. By morning, pilgrims had gathered. A shepherd claimed the words cured his blindness.

A noble claimed they proved his enemies damned. Merchants sold scraps of scorched cloth. Severin announced that the abbey alone could interpret the sign. The Gospel wrote another line: Every cage begins as an explanation.

Severin ordered Caldus to produce an authorized copy. “Remove what invites confusion.” “That may be all of it.” “Then preserve the useful danger and discard the rest.” Caldus sat before a clean sheet. For three days he could not write. Each time his pen touched parchment, the furnace showed him a face altered by his previous work.

A mother whose murdered sons had been renamed insurgents. A novice expelled for questioning a relic. A prisoner who confessed after reading Caldus’s illustrated torments. On the fourth night, a woman stepped from the heat.

Nema's library beneath the furnace contained no shelves because shelves implied categories the dead had never agreed upon. Testimonies drifted in ash like migrating birds. Caldus read a condemned astronomer's final calculations beside a widow's accusation against a saint, then a child's question about why divine justice resembled the king's prisons. None of the texts were automatically true merely because they had been suppressed. That discovery unsettled him. “I thought this place preserved truth,” he said. Nema corrected him: “It preserves what power removed. Truth still requires judgment.” The Furnace Gospel was not a new authority. It was an attack on the fantasy that authority could spare a person the labor of discernment.

Her robe was burned away at the edges, but her skin carried no wound. “Are you a demon?” Caldus asked. “Would that make the words false?” “It would reveal their source.” “Men who cannot answer a statement often examine the speaker.” She called herself Nema, keeper of discarded lines. Beneath the furnace, she said, lay every truth removed to make power appear holy.

The Gospel was not one book. It was the pressure of those buried words seeking form. “Why choose me?” “You know how beautiful a lie can be when properly illuminated.” Nema led him through the furnace. The flames were cold.

Beyond them stretched a library without walls. Books lay in ash, whispering. Caldus found histories written by the defeated, prayers that accused God, testimonies of women dismissed as temptation, discoveries condemned because they arrived before permission. At the center stood his own unfinished life.

He saw the boy he had been, curious and insolent, asking why an all-knowing God required monks to hide books. He saw Severin strike him. He saw himself learn that safety lived inside approved sentences. “I was afraid,” Caldus said.

“Fear is honest,” Nema replied. “What you built from it was not.” When he returned, the abbey was burning. Not with ordinary flame. Every false line glowed red while every honest uncertainty remained black.

Caldus found one page written by his own hand but never completed. Years earlier, a novice named Petr had confessed that he no longer believed a relic was genuine. Caldus had reported him to Severin. Petr was expelled in winter and later imprisoned for preaching without permission. In the buried page, Caldus had begun a letter defending the novice, then abandoned it before the ink dried. Nema asked why. “I wanted to remain useful,” he said. “To whom?” Caldus looked around the endless discarded library and understood that usefulness had been the respectable name he gave to fear.

The monks tore pages apart, desperate to separate obedience from deceit. Severin stood before the altar holding the authorized copy Caldus had not written. “Tell them it is a test,” the abbot pleaded. “Tell them faith requires submission.” “To what?” “To the Church.” “You used to say God.” For one instant, Severin looked old.

Caldus took the black pen. He wrote on the chapel wall: We do not know. The furnace roared. The words did not burn.

Some monks fled. Some attacked him. Others sat among the falling ash and began reading pages they had been forbidden to touch. Severin raised a torch to the original Gospel.

Nema caught his wrist. “You cannot destroy it,” she said. “Why?” “Because you are inside it.” The book opened to its final page. There stood the abbey, the furnace, the pilgrims, the merchants, Caldus with his pen, Severin with his torch.

After the abbey burned, the surviving monks argued for months about what had happened. Some called Caldus a prophet. Others called him destroyer. He rejected both titles. He and a handful of novices began copying texts again, but every volume included its sources, disputes, omissions, and uncertainties. Their pages were uglier than the old illuminated histories and harder to weaponize. Travelers complained that the new books gave no simple answers. Caldus considered that their first sign of honesty.

Beneath the image waited a blank line. Caldus understood. The Gospel did not predict. It demanded authorship.

He wrote: The truth did not save us. It made salvation impossible to counterfeit. The furnace went dark. Without its light, dawn entered through the windows.

It was weak, gray, and sufficient. Years later, travelers found the abbey roofless. No official history explained its fall. In the old scriptorium rested a book without a title.

Its pages were cool. Anyone could open it, but no two readers found the same words. At the beginning, however, one warning remained constant: Read only if you are willing to lose the lie that brought you here.
