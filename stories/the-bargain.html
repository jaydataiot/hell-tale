The Bargain

He expected horns. He found a ledger, a beautiful suit, and the exact language of every promise he had needed someone else to keep.

Approx. Time
February 22, moments before the midnight tearing of contracts.
Holiday
The Feast of Broken Vows.
Reader Note
This tale unfolds during the holiday's final rite, when promises are read aloud and destroyed.
First Descent

The man arrived in Hell with his hands folded as if he had been expecting an appointment. He expected claws. He expected horns. He expected the kind of vulgar spectacle he had purchased for other men in paintings and sermons.

Instead, he found a narrow room, a black table, and a figure in an elegant suit turning the pages of a ledger. The figure did not look up. “Before the end,” it said, “what did you ask for?” The man smiled. He had spent a lifetime surviving questions by answering the questioner instead.

“A name would help.” “Yours will be sufficient.” The ledger opened by itself. Millions of names moved across its pages like ants fleeing a fire. Beside every name stood a promise. Beside every promise stood a price.

His own name appeared in the center of the page. Lucian Voss had been called generous by men who needed his favor and ruthless by men who had lost it. Neither word pleased him. Generosity suggested weakness.

Ruthlessness suggested appetite. He preferred disciplined. He had inherited three factories, acquired nine more, purchased a newspaper, a senator, two bishops, and enough public affection to make all four arrangements respectable. “I asked for nothing,” Lucian said.

“I built what I possessed.” The figure finally raised its eyes. They were ordinary eyes, gray and tired. “Then this will be brief.” It turned the ledger toward him. The first promise had been made when Lucian was nine.

His father lay dying above a kitchen in winter. Lucian had knelt beside the bed and promised any listening power that he would never again be helpless. The room remembered his exact words, including the ones he had forgotten. Take tenderness, he had whispered.

Take sleep. Take everyone before they can leave me. Only do not let me become small. “A child’s fear is not a contract,” Lucian said.

Long before the black table, Lucian had learned that fear could be disguised as arithmetic. At fourteen he kept the accounts for the rooming house his mother managed after his father's death. He discovered that columns behaved better than people: a number did not cough through the night, beg for another week, or look ashamed when rent was late. When a tenant named Mr. Vale asked for mercy after losing two fingers at the rail yard, Lucian granted him seven days and then locked the room on the eighth. His mother called it cruel. Lucian called it consistency. Years later, when investors praised his discipline, he remembered the padlock rather than the man's face. The suited figure found that memory without turning a page. “You practiced early,” it said. Lucian objected that poverty had taught him necessity. “Necessity taught you fear,” the figure replied. “You taught fear to wear a necktie.”

“Correct.” The entry vanished. Relief warmed him. “Then you admit the error.” “I admit your first petition carried no price. What followed was your own work.” The wall behind the figure opened onto a mill floor.

Women stood at looms beneath windows blackened by coal. A boy reached into a machine while the belt still moved. Lucian recognized the room. He had toured it once.

He had complained that the workers stared. “Industrial necessity,” he said. The boy’s hand disappeared between the gears. The sound was small.

That was what Lucian remembered now. Not the scream, but the small sound before it. The ledger recorded a meeting held the following morning. Compensation had been denied because the boy’s mother could not prove the child had been instructed to clean the teeth while the machine was still.

“I did not write that rule.” “No. You approved the man who did.” The next page showed a river carrying red dye past a village well. Another showed police breaking a line of hungry workers. Another showed a widow signing away her house beneath the gaze of Lucian’s attorney.

In each account, his hand was distant. In each account, his profit arrived clean. Lucian began to understand the room. “This is an accusation.” “It is an inventory.” “Where is my advocate?” “You employed them all.” The black door opened.

His chief counsel entered carrying a leather case. Harrow had died twelve years earlier and looked healthier than Lucian remembered. “At last,” Lucian said. Harrow placed the case on the table and took the chair beside him.

“I have been retained by the opposing interests.” “With what money?” “None. They paid me with memory.” The case contained every contract Harrow had drawn for Lucian. The language remained crisp. The consequences bled through it.

The mill scene did not vanish when Lucian looked away. Instead, the room widened and forced him into the meeting held after the boy's injury. He saw himself younger, impatient, drumming two fingers on a walnut table while managers argued over compensation. Harrow had quietly warned that paying the family might establish a precedent. The foreman had suggested replacing the missing guard on the machine. Lucian remembered asking what the guard would cost across all twelve factories. The answer had been less than the flowers later sent to his mother's memorial. He had still postponed the repair until the next quarter. Now the injured boy sat at the far end of the conference table, older than he had ever lived to become, holding the hand he never got to keep. “Which part was necessity?” the boy asked. Lucian searched for the manager who had made the recommendation, but Hell had removed every subordinate from the room. There was only Lucian, the number, and the choice.

A harmless paragraph smelled of a family sleeping beneath a bridge. A signature line coughed with the lungs of miners. An indemnity clause contained the final thoughts of thirty-seven passengers on a ferry built from rejected steel. Lucian pushed the papers away.

“All commerce has consequences.” “Yes,” said the figure. “That is why commerce is counted.” “I gave millions to hospitals.” A second ledger appeared. It was thinner. His charities were entered there without mockery: the fever ward, the orphan school, the bread kitchens, the scholarships carrying his name.

Lucian leaned closer. “You see.” “We see everything.” Each gift bore another column. The fever ward had been built after his mills poisoned the river. The orphan school housed children whose fathers had died in his mines.

The bread kitchens opened during the strike he prolonged. Even so, some good remained. A girl had learned medicine. A sick man had lived long enough to reconcile with his son.

A cook had stolen bread from the kitchen and fed a street Lucian never knew existed. “Those lives belong to my account.” The figure shook its head. “You wish to own the mercy of people who repaired what you broke.” For the first time, Lucian struck the table. The room trembled, but the figure did not.

“What bargain do you want?” he asked. “The one you always wanted. Your suffering can be transferred.” Lucian became still. Six people were brought into the room.

His wife. His eldest son. Harrow. The bishop whose cathedral bore his mother’s name.

Before the six possible substitutes entered, Harrow asked permission to open one final file. It contained no contract, only letters Lucian had never read: petitions from workers, warnings from engineers, a note from his wife after their youngest child's funeral, and a page written by his son at school describing a father who could purchase anything except an afternoon. Lucian's anger became almost physical. He accused Harrow of withholding the letters. Harrow answered that each had been placed on Lucian's desk. “You trained us to decide what was worth your attention,” he said. “Eventually we learned that pain without financial consequence was not.” For the first time Lucian understood delegation as something more intimate than management. He had not merely hired people to perform tasks. He had hired them to stand between himself and knowledge. The bargain offered by Hell was therefore perfectly tailored: one more intermediary, one final transfer, one last clean signature.

The mill foreman. Last came an old woman in a patched coat. Lucian did not recognize her. “Choose one,” said the figure.

“That soul will serve your sentence. You may depart.” Harrow stared at the floor. The bishop began to pray. Lucian’s son wept with the embarrassed silence he had learned at boarding school.

His wife only watched him. Lucian’s mind resumed its old work. Harrow had profited. The foreman had enforced.

The bishop had blessed. His son had squandered. His wife had known. Any one of them could be made responsible without changing the facts very much.

“Who is she?” he asked, pointing to the old woman. The figure read from the ledger. “Marta Elian. Her husband died in your eastern mine.

Her daughter died in the fever ward. Her grandson was the boy at the gears.” “Why is she here?” “To make the offer complete.” The old woman stepped forward. “Choose me.” Lucian distrusted willingness. “Why?” “Because then you will leave.” He almost laughed.

“You forgive me?” “No. I want to see whether there is anywhere you can go where you are not yourself.” The sentence struck deeper than accusation. Lucian studied the faces before him and discovered that every choice was familiar. His life had been one long room in which another person stood between him and cost.

The bargain was not Hell’s invention. Hell had merely removed the paperwork. “If I refuse?” “You will serve what belongs to you.” “For how long?” “Until you can feel a consequence without converting it into a number.” Lucian looked again at Marta. Her hands were knotted with age.

When Lucian finally chose his own account, the sentence began not with agony but with proximity. He became the boy at the gears and the executive reading the accident report. He became Marta waiting outside the mine and the clerk stamping her claim denied. He became a passenger on the ferry and the investor pleased that cheaper steel had improved the quarter. Each perspective arrived complete, with memories and private hopes that had never appeared in his ledgers. The punishment was not that he felt pain multiplied by thousands. It was that each person ceased to be interchangeable. Every statistic acquired a kitchen, a favorite song, a child, an unfinished argument, a morning plan. The world he had compressed into columns expanded until there was nowhere left to stand outside it.

He imagined those hands inside the machinery of his sentence. He imagined the door opening. He imagined daylight, or whatever passed for it after death. “I choose—” His wife closed her eyes.

His son bent at the waist. Harrow waited like a clerk beside a signature. Lucian stopped. The old childhood prayer returned to him: Take everyone before they can leave me.

He understood at last that it had been answered. Not by a demon, but by every choice he had made afterward. “I choose my account,” he said. The figure closed the ledger.

The mill floor rose around him. The belt moved. The river darkened. Thirty-seven passengers called from cold water.

He felt no flame. He felt each distance disappear. Marta watched until he began to scream. Then she turned to the suited figure.

“Will he learn?” “Every soul learns,” it said. “The question is what remains when the lesson is no longer avoidable.” The door opened for her. Behind them, Lucian Voss received the first thing he had ever truly purchased. He received the price.
