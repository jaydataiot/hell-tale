Tales From Below · Tale II

The Pious

He spent a life teaching people where to kneel. Below, the doors remember who bent, who broke, and who was told to call it grace.

Approx. Time
October 13, at the ninth bell after the cathedral funeral begins.
Holiday
The Feast of the Scapegoat.
Reader Note
The holiday sharpens the accusation: one celebrated man is asked who benefited when others carried his sins.
First Descent

The bishop died beneath a ceiling painted with angels. His followers filled the cathedral for the funeral. They praised his certainty, his discipline, and the way his voice made doubt seem like a disease from which only obedience could save them. He entered the next world wearing the same small, confident smile carved into the memorial prepared before his death.

Then the light changed. He stood in a corridor lined with church doors. Behind each door waited the face of someone he had corrected, shamed, exiled, silenced, or used as proof of his own goodness. A clerk in ash-colored robes offered him a form.

“State your name as you gave it to the poor,” she said, “not as you printed it on the building.” “Bishop Aurelius Vane.” The clerk waited. “His Excellency, the Most Reverend—” “The poor did not call you that.” He remembered the bread line behind Saint Orison’s. The people there called him Your Grace when cameras were present. When the cameras left, he entered through another door.

“Father,” he said. The form remained blank. The first door opened. A young woman sat inside a bare room holding a dead infant.

Aurelius knew her only after she spoke. “You called me impure.” Lena Marr had come to him at seventeen, pregnant by a deacon whose name the bishop removed from the inquiry. The church had sent the deacon elsewhere and sent Lena away. Her child died during the winter.

“I upheld the teaching,” Aurelius said. The clerk made a mark. “You have named the shield. We asked for the hand.” Door after door opened.

Aurelius had not always loved certainty. As a young seminarian he had once filled notebooks with questions and hidden them beneath his mattress. Mother Sabine found one and, instead of reporting him, wrote a sentence on the final page: Faith that cannot survive a question is only fear with architecture. He treasured the line for years. Then came advancement, donors, committees, and the intoxicating discovery that people rewarded a man who sounded certain. Questions complicated sermons. Certainty filled pews. By forty, Aurelius no longer hid his doubts; he had trained himself not to recognize them. The corridor returned the old notebook to his hands. Every question he had crossed out now appeared in the margins of another person's life.

Brother Tomas, who confessed doubt and lost his parish. Elias Crow, who slept on the cathedral steps while an empty rectory stood heated. Sister Amabel, whose research Aurelius published beneath his own name. A choirboy who had told the truth about a beloved priest and learned how quickly holy men could call truth a scandal.

Aurelius supplied reasons for each. Doctrine. Order. Prudence.

The protection of the faithful. He had used these words so often they came polished. At the corridor’s end stood a chapel smaller than a closet. Inside waited an altar, a basin, and a single chair.

“You may wash,” said the clerk. The bishop plunged his hands into the basin. The water blackened. “Again.” He washed until the skin split.

The water grew darker. “This is blasphemous theater.” “No. Theater requires an audience that can be deceived.” The wall behind the altar brightened. Aurelius saw his cathedral during the years of his rule.

He saw himself blessing the homeless at Christmas, then ordering iron spikes placed beneath the portico in January. He saw letters from desperate families stacked unopened beneath a silver reliquary. He saw the private dining room where he discussed humility over wine that cost more than a laborer’s year. Yet the vision also showed what he had forgotten: nights beside dying parishioners, a scholarship quietly paid, a frightened child carried from a flood.

Behind one door waited not a victim but a meeting. Aurelius saw the cathedral board gathered around polished oak while Lena's accusation against the deacon lay in a folder. A lawyer warned of scandal. A donor warned of division. Aurelius remembered the strange calm that entered him when the solution became institutional rather than moral. Transfer the deacon. Offer Lena counseling somewhere else. Avoid language that admitted wrongdoing. He had prayed before signing the plan and had mistaken the relief he felt afterward for divine approval. The clerk stopped the memory at the moment his pen touched paper. “What revelation occurred here?” she asked. Aurelius said they had protected the church. “From what?” “Scandal.” The clerk looked toward Lena. “You mean from being known.”

He reached toward these memories. “There. You see I served.” “You did,” said the clerk. The admission confused him more than condemnation.

“Then why am I here?” “Because good deeds do not become currency with which cruelty is purchased.” Another figure entered the chapel. She wore the plain dress of an old sacristan. Aurelius recognized Mother Sabine, who had taught him before ambition taught him better lessons. “You told me pride was the first sin,” he said.

“I told you pride begins when a man mistakes his office for his soul.” She placed his mitre on the altar. It had grown enormous, crusted with gold, too heavy for any neck. “The faithful needed symbols.” “They needed courage,” Sabine replied. “You taught them dependence and called it faith.” The chapel doors vanished.

In their place stood the congregation from his funeral. Thousands listened as a recording of his final sermon filled the corridor. He had preached that suffering was a gate God opened for those He loved. Lena’s dead child cried behind the words.

“Stop this.” The clerk handed him a key. “You may stop one voice. Choose.” The sermon continued. So did the infant.

Mother Sabine led him through a side passage filled with discarded objects: wedding rings returned after annulments, letters from expelled clergy, children's drawings left in empty classrooms, protest signs collected after police cleared cathedral steps. Aurelius recognized none of them, yet his seal appeared on the orders that had moved each object out of sight. Sabine explained that institutions develop a second theology composed entirely of what they refuse to see. “You preached a God who saw everything,” she said, “while building a church skilled at looking away.” Aurelius tried to answer with the good he had done, but the corridor would not permit subtraction. Mercy and cruelty remained side by side, both true, neither canceling the other.

Around them rose every prayer Aurelius had ignored because answering it would inconvenience someone powerful. He chose the recording of himself. The key dissolved. Silence did not follow.

Without his voice, he could hear the others clearly. For a long time he knelt. He began with Lena’s name. Then Tomas.

Elias. Amabel. The choirboy, whose name he had trained himself not to remember. “Jonas,” said the clerk.

“Jonas.” With each name, a church door opened. Behind none waited torture. There waited only the life he had refused to imagine. He felt hunger without the lesson he once attached to it.

Shame without the sermon. Loneliness without the promise that it improved the lonely. When the last door opened, Aurelius was no longer wearing vestments. He stood in the thin nightclothes of the old man who had died beneath painted angels.

“Am I damned?” he asked. “You came asking for a kingdom where every person knew his place,” said Sabine. “You have found it.” The cathedral appeared around him. Its nave was full, but the pulpit stood at floor level.

When the congregation finally spoke from the galleries, they did not deliver a unified indictment. Some thanked him. A dying man remembered Aurelius holding his hand. A scholarship student said the bishop's quiet gift had changed her life. Then Jonas spoke about the price of telling the truth, and Lena spoke about winter. The contradictions tormented Aurelius more than simple condemnation could have. He wanted a verdict that would tell him what kind of man he had been. The Tribunal offered none. He had been generous and cowardly, tender and vain, disciplined and cruel. His first real confession began when he stopped trying to reduce a life to a single moral adjective.

The people he had silenced sat above him in the galleries. Before him lay no prepared text. “What must I preach?” “Nothing.” The clerk removed his ring. Sabine removed his cross.

Lena placed the dead child in his arms. “Listen,” she said. The bishop held the weight he had reduced to doctrine. The congregation began to speak, one soul at a time.

They did not speak forever. It only seemed so because Aurelius had never allowed them a minute. At last the infant opened its eyes. The bishop looked down and saw no accusation there, only a question too simple for theology.

What did you do with the power you were given? Aurelius bowed his uncovered head. For the first time in his long and pious life, he had no answer prepared. That was the beginning of his confession.
