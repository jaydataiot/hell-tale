Tales From Below · Tale VI

The Last Sermon

At 3:17, the preacher meets the voice he spent forty years inventing. It has notes.

Approx. Time
December 24 at 3:17 a.m., continuing toward the next infernal dawn.
Holiday
Unholy Night.
Holiday Shift
If the visitation lasts through midnight, the story crosses into Christmas in Hell, when arguments over sacred meaning become part of the punishment.
First Descent

The preacher woke at 3:17 in the morning and found a visitor sitting in the chair beside his bed. “Leave it dark,” the visitor said. “You always preferred me that way.” Reverend Silas Creed knew the voice. He had invented it for forty years.

He had given it laughter, hunger, silk, smoke, and a hatred of children. He had made it whisper through records played backward and wait behind every question a frightened believer was afraid to ask. “Satan.” “You got several details wrong.” Lightning opened the room. For an instant Silas saw no beast, only a grave man with tired eyes and rain on his shoulders.

“Why are you here?” “Before you meet the people who believed you.” Silas reached for the lamp. It would not light. His wife slept beside him without breathing, though she was not dead. The clock remained at 3:17.

“This is a dream.” “That has never prevented you from calling something revelation.” The visitor placed six cassette tapes on the blanket. Silas recognized the labels. Each held a sermon from a different decade. The Devil in the Nursery.

The War Against Doubt. God’s Chosen Nation. The Obedient Wife. The Mark Among Us.

Silas Creed had discovered the economics of fear by accident. In his first church, attendance doubled after a sermon about demonic influence in popular music. The offering doubled too. An elder congratulated him on “speaking boldly,” and Silas felt the peculiar warmth of being rewarded for certainty. He told himself the crowd had responded to truth. Years later, his staff would test sermon titles in advance, tracking which threats increased attendance, donations, and mail orders. They never called it marketing. They called it discernment.

The Last Sermon, still unwritten. “You made a career of my biography,” Satan said. “I thought professional courtesy required a correction.” The first tape began to play without a machine. Silas’s younger voice filled the room, fierce and bright.

He warned a small congregation that doubt opened the soul like a window. The wall became that church. A twelve-year-old boy listened from the second row. Years later, the boy would hide questions about his own nature until shame became a rope in a garage.

“I never told him to die.” “No. You only told him what would happen if he lived honestly.” The second tape played. Silas preached that suffering women must submit with joy. His own daughter Miriam sat in the front pew with a bruise beneath her sleeve.

She had come home that night and asked whether God required her to return to her husband. Silas remembered his answer. Marriage is a cross. Miriam returned.

Three years later, the husband broke her jaw. Silas paid the hospital bill anonymously and praised forgiveness publicly. “She survived.” “You say that as if survival proves the wound was permitted.” Silas tried to pray. Every holy name came out wearing his own voice.

The visitor made Silas listen to a tape he did not remember labeling. It contained no sermon, only backstage conversations recorded over an old cassette. Silas heard himself asking whether the story about a possessed teenager had been verified. A producer answered that verification would weaken the segment. Another voice suggested changing “claimed to see” into “saw.” Silas laughed and approved it. In the bedroom, he demanded to know why such small editorial choices mattered. Satan replied, “Because you taught millions of people to fear evidence less than confidence.”

The visitor leaned forward. “That is your first correction. I did not invent fear. I merely refused to call it faith.” The third tape contained a rally.

Flags hung behind the cross. Silas declared one nation chosen and its enemies instruments of darkness. Young men cheered. Some enlisted.

One came home without legs; another did not come home. “I supported my country.” “You made God small enough to salute it.” “People needed certainty.” “People wanted certainty. You needed their need.” The room expanded into Silas’s private office. Donation charts covered the walls.

Beside each sermon title stood the amount it had raised. Fear performed best. Satan appeared in red ink wherever revenue had fallen. Silas saw the pattern and hated the neatness of it.

Miriam's appearance broke something the tapes could not. Silas remembered her as difficult during those years, argumentative and spiritually cold. Now the room showed what his memory had edited out: the way she flinched when her husband raised his voice, the long sleeves in summer, the half-packed suitcase in her car. Silas had noticed each detail separately and refused to assemble them into knowledge. “I thought keeping the marriage together might save him too,” he said. Miriam answered, “You were willing to use me as the treatment.” There was no theological phrase left that could make the sentence smaller.

“You think I did no good?” The visitor shook his head. “That is another detail you got wrong. Hell does not require you to be entirely evil. Only children’s stories do.” They watched Silas feed families, comfort widows, build a clinic, sit all night with a dying friend.

None was removed. None canceled what followed. His life refused the simplicity he had sold to others. At 3:17, the bedroom door opened.

Miriam entered. She was alive, older now, and carrying the silence that had grown between them. “Is this really her?” Silas asked. “It is really what you know of her.

The difference should trouble you.” Miriam sat at the foot of the bed. “Why didn’t you believe me?” “I believed you.” “Then why did you send me back?” He reached for doctrine and found every phrase already used. “I was afraid the church would divide.” “It did. It divided me from myself.” She vanished when the fourth tape began.

On it, Silas described Hell in loving detail. Hooks. Wheels. Lakes of fire.

Before dawn, Satan showed Silas one final audience: people who had heard his sermons and rejected them. A teacher who told a terrified student that questions were not possession. A mother who left Silas's church after he condemned her son and chose the son. A veteran who stopped using divine language to excuse what he had done in war. Silas was surprised to feel offended that goodness had grown in opposition to him. Satan noticed. “You still want to own every mercy that happened near your name.” The lesson echoed Lucian Voss's ledger, though Silas had never known the man.

He had noticed early that congregations leaned closer to punishment than grace. “Were any details correct?” he asked. “The pain.” “And the flames?” “Sometimes.” “The demons?” “Frequently human.” “What happens there?” “Nothing is hidden well enough to remain harmless.” Silas looked at the blank sixth cassette. “My last sermon.” “You will preach it tomorrow.” “If this is judgment, why send me back?” “Because a warning after death is only punishment.

A warning before death is choice.” The visitor gave him the tape. It was warm. “What must I say?” “What you know.” “They will leave.” “Some.” “The ministry will collapse.” “Perhaps.” “My enemies will rejoice.” Satan smiled without kindness. “You still believe truth is valuable only when the right people benefit.” The clock moved to 3:18.

His wife drew a breath. The lamp came on. Silas was alone. The next morning the sanctuary overflowed.

News of his coming retirement had spread. Cameras waited. The choir sang the anthem chosen to summarize his life. Behind the pulpit, the screens displayed photographs: crusades, baptisms, presidents, hospitals, crowds with raised hands.

The morning of the final sermon, Silas's staff sensed something was wrong. His media director handed him the approved retirement message. An elder warned that donors expected gratitude, not controversy. His wife asked whether he was ill. Silas looked at the polished hallway lined with photographs of himself beside presidents, athletes, and grieving families. For decades the building had reflected his voice back to him until echo felt like agreement. He entered the sanctuary carrying the blank cassette because, for the first time, he had no idea whether telling the truth would leave anything standing.

Silas carried no Bible and no notes. In his pocket lay the blank cassette. “For forty years,” he began, “I have warned you about the father of lies.” The congregation leaned forward. “I did not tell you how often a lie wears the face of a frightened preacher.” No one moved.

He told them about Miriam. He named the boy from the second row. He described the donation charts, the calculated terrors, the private doubts he had condemned in public. He did not decorate the confession with a story of redemption.

He did not ask forgiveness before the wounded had decided whether it belonged to him. People left. Others shouted. An elder cut the microphone, but Silas continued in the sudden honest quiet.

At the rear doors stood the visitor in the dark coat. Silas reached the final sentence. “If God is truth, then every comfortable lie preached in His defense is an accusation against Him.” The visitor bowed his head. Miriam remained in the front pew.

She did not smile. She did not leave. Afterward, Silas found the cassette in his pocket. A label had appeared in red ink: THE FIRST HONEST SERMON.

In the seven months after the sermon, the ministry did collapse. Lawsuits arrived. Former staff released documents. Supporters called Silas betrayed, senile, courageous, possessed, repentant, and opportunistic. He refused interviews. Instead he spent his remaining time answering letters from people who described what his words had done. Some thanked him. Some cursed him. Some wanted money. Some wanted only acknowledgment. He learned that apology was not a speech but a practice of remaining present after the part where one wanted to be forgiven. Miriam never told him everything was repaired. She gave him the spare room anyway.

He died seven months later, not beneath painted angels or before a cheering crowd, but in Miriam’s spare room with the window open. At 3:17 in the morning, he entered a narrow hall. The people who had believed him waited there. Some held gratitude.

Some held injuries. Most held both. Satan opened the door. “Am I in Hell?” Silas asked.

“You are where your words became lives.” “How long must I stay?” “Until you have listened to every sermon you made another person live.” Silas stepped inside. For the first time, no pulpit stood between him and the congregation. He took an empty chair. And the last sermon began.
