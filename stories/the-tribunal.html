Tales From Below · Tale IV

The Tribunal

They arrive in rows, still looking for the pastor. The benches are not pews. The floor has begun remembering names.

Approx. Time
November 13, first docket of the Brazen Tribunal.
Holiday
The Tribunal of Saints.
Reader Note
The congregation arrives on the day Hell traditionally audits holy reputations against actual conduct.
First Descent

The congregation died together. The roof collapsed during the final hymn, burying the faithful beneath stone saints and splintered beams. They arrived below still arranged in rows, still looking toward the front for someone with authority to explain the delay. No one came.

The benches on which they sat were witness stands. Around them rose thirteen tiers of dark seats. At the center lay a circle of smoked glass showing the faces of people they had prayed for publicly and abandoned privately. A voice moved through the chamber.

“Titles are surrendered. Excuses will be heard. None will be presumed true.” Reverend Pell stood first. Death had left dust in his hair and a hymnbook crushed against his chest.

“My flock acted in good faith.” Six judges entered from six doors. Their faces changed whenever the congregation blinked: child, widow, stranger, enemy, beloved, self. Behind them came seven Persecutors carrying iron hooks shaped like questions. Thirteen Witnesses occupied the first tier.

Six hundred thirty-nine Jurors filled the rest. No two looked in the same direction. At the circle’s edge waited the Sentencer, veiled and silent. “Who is accused?” Pell asked.

The smoked glass brightened beneath every bench. “Whoever recognizes himself.” The first case concerned the winter shelter. The congregation had raised money, posed for photographs, and closed it after neighbors complained that poverty lowered property values. A man named Abram had frozen behind their fellowship hall.

Before the first case, the congregation tried to restore the order of Sunday morning. Ushers assigned benches. Elders whispered about procedure. Reverend Pell asked whether the choir should remain together. The Tribunal ignored them. Their habits survived death because hierarchy had become muscle memory. Ruth alone noticed that the benches were arranged in a circle rather than rows. There was no front. Every seat faced another seat. “Perhaps that is the point,” she murmured. No one heard her, which was also the point.

Pell raised his Bible. “We did what resources allowed.” One Persecutor hooked the word resources from his mouth. It hung in the air, opened, and spilled receipts for new stained glass. The Jurors made no sound.

Their silence weighed more than outrage. Next came Mara Finch, who led the women’s charity. She had visited the sick and carried casseroles to every funeral. She had also spread a rumor about a young mother until the woman fled town.

“I was concerned for the children.” A hook removed concerned. Inside it crawled pleasure. Mara screamed that this was unfair. The judges changed faces.

One became the young mother. Another became Mara at seventeen, weeping after a rumor of her own. Case followed case. Some were small enough to be forgotten and sharp enough to change a life.

A withheld invitation. A prayer request used as gossip. A son condemned for loving the wrong person. A lonely old man treated kindly only while his estate remained undecided.

The winter shelter case unfolded beyond the photograph everyone remembered. The glass showed committee meetings where compassion slowly became inconvenience. At first they debated staffing. Then insurance. Then parking. Finally a developer warned that visible poverty might affect the church's expansion campaign. No one said Abram should freeze. That sentence was never spoken. Instead, responsibility dissolved across twelve reasonable objections until no single person felt cruel enough to stop the result. The Tribunal replayed the final vote without sound. Hands rose politely. Coffee cooled. Outside, Abram folded newspaper beneath his coat.

The congregation began accusing one another. Fingers rose. Alliances formed by pew and family. Every soul believed the sins of the next bench provided useful scale.

The fifth judge rang a cracked bell. “Comparison is not repentance.” The floor turned transparent. Beneath it waited the church as it had been before the collapse. Rescue crews moved through dust.

Outside, reporters interviewed survivors. Deacon Vale, who had stepped out to answer a private call, praised Reverend Pell’s leadership. No one knew Pell had approved cheaper repairs after an engineer warned the roof would fail. The engineer appeared among the Witnesses.

Pell’s hymnbook fell open. Inside was the letter. He had kept it to remind himself to act after the building campaign. “I intended—” The seventh Persecutor drew intended from him.

It was hollow. The congregation surged away. They had called him shepherd in life. Now they called him murderer.

Pell shouted above them. “You voted for the new organ. You applauded the expansion. You asked me not to alarm anyone.” The glass reflected all their faces.

Mara Finch's case became the congregation's first open fracture. She insisted that gossip was too small a sin for such a court, and several people agreed until the glass showed the rumor traveling from prayer circle to text message to school pickup line. Each person added only a little. By the time it reached the young mother's employer, the story had become an accusation no one could trace to a source. She lost her job, then her lease, then the fragile custody arrangement Mara had claimed to protect. The Persecutor placed the original sentence beside the final consequence. The distance between them was enormous. “Small acts are not always small after they leave you,” said one Judge.

For once, he was telling the truth. The Tribunal recessed. The damned received no water, but thirst was not their suffering. Their suffering was that conversation continued without allowing anyone to leave.

Families repeated arguments cut short by death. Friends confessed what they had concealed. Kindnesses emerged beside cruelties, complicating every hatred. An old woman named Ruth sat alone.

She had cleaned the church for forty years and spoken rarely. In life they had mistaken quiet for agreement. “What do the Jurors decide?” she asked the nearest Witness. “They do not decide.” “Then why are they here?” “To prevent any soul from imagining that consequence has only one observer.” When the judges returned, Ruth entered the center voluntarily.

The glass showed her leaving food outside Abram’s hiding place after the shelter closed. It also showed her seeing the engineer’s letter on Pell’s desk and saying nothing because she feared losing her employment. “I was afraid,” she said. No hook came for the word.

It was true. “Does fear absolve you?” “No.” “Does it condemn you?” Ruth looked at Abram among the Witnesses. “It explains me. It does not repair him.” For the first time, the Sentencer lifted its head.

During the recess, Pell moved among the dead trying to rebuild loyalty. He apologized selectively, reminded people of baptisms and weddings, and suggested that the roof failure had been a collective decision. Some listened because even in Hell it was comforting to have a shepherd. Ruth watched him recruit agreement and finally interrupted. “You are doing it again.” Pell demanded to know what she meant. “Turning us into a wall between you and what you know.” The words left him furious because they were plain enough to survive every theological defense.

One by one, the congregation entered the circle. The proceeding lasted beyond clocks. Those who defended every act found their words returned as rooms they could not exit. Those who confessed merely to shorten pain discovered that confession used as a key opened nothing.

Those who named harm without bargaining were seated beside the people they harmed and made to hear the part of the story in which they were not the hero. At last only Reverend Pell remained. He stood upon the smoked glass. Above him, six Judges watched.

Around him, seven Persecutors lowered their hooks. Thirteen Witnesses waited. Six hundred thirty-nine Jurors breathed as one body. “I failed them,” Pell said.

The words lay lifeless. “I loved the church.” The glass showed stone, organ pipes, his name on a bronze plaque. “I loved being needed.” The glass warmed. “I knew the roof was dangerous.” It grew clear.

“I chose the appearance of safety over the cost of truth.” At last he could see beneath it, not the ruined sanctuary but the people as they had been on the morning of their deaths: impatient, tender, vain, frightened, generous, cruel, alive. The Sentencer removed its veil. It wore Pell’s face. His sentence was not flame.

When Pell lifted the first stone of his sentence, the sanctuary did not appear as a ruin but as a construction site at dawn. The tools were ordinary. The plans were the engineer's original plans, the ones he had postponed. Each stone carried a name. Some belonged to people killed in the collapse; others belonged to those harmed by decisions made in the church long before the roof failed. Pell understood that rebuilding would require more than masonry. Every time he was tempted to make the structure impressive, the foundation cracked. It would stand only when he built for the people who needed shelter rather than the people whose admiration he needed.

He would rebuild the sanctuary alone, stone by stone, while feeling the weight of every life lost beneath it. When the roof was sound, the congregation could choose whether to enter. He could not ask them. He could not preach.

He could only build. “How long?” Pell whispered. Ruth answered from the first tier. “Until the shelter stays open.” The Tribunal emptied.

The six Judges became doors. The seven Persecutors became keys. The Witnesses departed with their memories intact. The Jurors vanished into the countless places where private choices become public ruin.

Reverend Pell lifted the first stone. It was heavier than doctrine.
