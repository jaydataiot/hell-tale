Tales From Below · Tale III

The Accuser

Heaven brings its oldest suspect to court. Humanity arrives as evidence and, inconveniently, as coauthor.

Approx. Time
August 19, during the opening summons of the Court Above.
Holiday
The Day of the Accused.
Reader Note
No holiday change occurs in this opening. The whole proceeding begins under a day devoted to contested blame.
First Descent

The summons was written in light. It burned through Satan’s desk, the stack of unfinished indictments beneath it, and the hand of the messenger who delivered it. The messenger did not complain. Angels had been trained to regard pain as proof that an order mattered.

Satan read the first line and sighed. “Again?” The Court Above had charged humanity with another century of crimes. War. Famine.

Cruelty made efficient. Faith converted into permission. The prosecution had assembled mountains of evidence and left one chair empty. The chair was for the Accuser.

Satan arrived without horns. This disappointed the gallery. He wore a dark coat and carried the ruined summons folded in his pocket. Above the bench hung a sun that gave light without warmth.

The Presiding Virtue spoke with seven mouths. “You are called to testify concerning the corruption of humankind.” “I am called whenever Heaven wants a second signature.” Murmurs moved among the saints. The chief prosecutor unfurled a scroll across the floor. It ran through the doors and down the stairs beyond sight.

The Court Above had summoned Satan many times, but this hearing felt different. In the antechamber he passed statues of civilizations that had each declared themselves permanent. Their inscriptions had been revised so often that the stone beneath the gold leaf was almost gone. An angelic clerk offered him a prepared statement admitting broad responsibility for human corruption. Satan read it, crossed out the word responsibility, and replaced it with usefulness. “That is not the approved language,” the clerk said. “That is why I improved it.” He had learned that Heaven's bureaucracy feared imprecision less than it feared unsanctioned precision.

“Do you deny tempting them?” “Often.” “Do you deny teaching them disobedience?” “Proudly.” “Do you deny your hand in murder, conquest, greed, and desecration?” Satan examined the empty chair before sitting. “Which hand?” The first witness was a king who had burned twelve cities while priests sang behind him. He pointed at Satan. “He filled me with ambition.” “You sent me away three times,” Satan said.

The king blinked. “The first time, I suggested you spare the eastern city so its rebellion would shame your father. You called mercy cowardice. The second, I offered you a dream in which your enemy’s children carried your name.

You ordered their deaths before breakfast. The third, I stopped speaking. You seemed to have mastered the craft.” The prosecutor objected. “The witness could not know the source of his thoughts.” “Then why was he permitted to blame me for them?” The court recorded the question and sealed it where no one could answer.

Next came a merchant who starved a province. Then an inquisitor. Then a mother who taught her son to hate strangers and wept when the hatred returned wearing a uniform. Each named a whisper, a shadow, an influence.

The king's testimony opened a procession of convenient memories. Each witness remembered temptation vividly and decision vaguely. Satan forced the court to replay the hours surrounding their crimes. The merchant who starved a province had received reports showing full granaries while children died outside them. The inquisitor had laughed with friends before signing warrants he later described as solemn burdens. The mother who taught hatred had once been corrected by her own child and chosen humiliation over curiosity. Satan did not absolve them. He made their agency impossible to outsource. The gallery grew angrier with every restored detail because responsibility, unlike evil, could not be safely exiled to a monster.

Satan remembered some of them. Others he had never met. At noon the Presiding Virtue called a recess. Satan walked onto a balcony overlooking Creation.

From that height, suffering appeared orderly. Smoke rose in straight lines. Graves resembled punctuation. An angel joined him.

The messenger’s burned hand had healed. “Why do you defend them?” the angel asked. “I do not.” “You dispute every charge.” “I dispute the convenience.” Below, a child stole bread. A magistrate sentenced him.

A wealthy woman praised the law. None required supernatural instruction. “If they are free,” Satan said, “their crimes belong to them. If they are not, the crimes belong to their maker.

During recess, the burned-handed messenger admitted that he had once carried an order to a city scheduled for destruction. “I asked whether the children were included,” he said. “They told me the question proved I was becoming susceptible to your influence.” Satan studied him. “And what did you do?” The angel looked down at the healed hand. “I delivered it.” The admission hung between them. Satan did not offer comfort. “Then remember the difference between obedience and innocence.” The angel asked whether Hell kept records of angels. Satan answered that Hell kept whatever Heaven discarded.

Heaven cannot have both conclusions merely because both are useful.” The angel looked afraid. “That is not permitted reasoning.” “It is the only kind worth permitting.” When court resumed, Satan called his own witnesses. The prosecutor protested that the Accuser had no right to mount a defense. “I am not defending humanity,” Satan said.

“I am indicting authorship.” He called Silence first. It entered as an empty robe. Silence had stood in camps, bedrooms, prisons, and churches while victims begged for any sign that the universe objected. He called Obedience.

It arrived wearing every uniform ever blessed. He called Certainty, blindfolded and carrying a torch. Last he called a nameless woman from a ruined city. She had lost her family in a war fought by men who claimed both Heaven and Hell desired it.

Still, she had hidden an enemy child beneath her floor. “Who tempted you to mercy?” Satan asked. “No one.” “Who commanded it?” “No one.” “Why did you act?” “The child was afraid.” Satan turned to the bench. “There is your corruption.

A creature capable of uncommanded mercy is also capable of uncommanded cruelty.” The seven mouths conferred. The sun above them dimmed. “What judgment do you seek?” “Accuracy.” The word unsettled the court. Satan removed from his coat a book bound in scorched leather.

When Satan called Silence, Obedience, and Certainty, the prosecution attempted to classify them as metaphors and therefore inadmissible. The Presiding Virtue overruled the objection because the court had spent centuries prosecuting humanity with equally abstract sins. Silence testified without speaking: the chamber filled with unanswered prayers, reports buried by institutions, neighbors who heard violence through walls, soldiers who knew orders were wrong, congregations that sensed cruelty and applauded anyway. Obedience then unfolded its uniforms across the floor. Certainty raised its torch, and every faction in the gallery insisted the flame belonged exclusively to its enemies. The nameless woman's mercy was the only evidence that required no doctrine to explain it.

It contained every deed attributed to him. He opened it and began returning the entries. A massacre moved to a general’s account. A betrayal moved to a friend’s.

A doctrine of hatred moved to the theologian who composed it and the congregations that enjoyed repeating it. Page by page, the book grew lighter while humanity’s record grew honest. Not innocent. Honest.

When he finished, only a few entries remained: questions planted, doors opened, forbidden fruit named forbidden so that desire could recognize itself. “You admit these?” asked the Virtue. “Every one.” “Then you are still the Accuser.” Satan stood. “No.

I was the first witness for the defense.” The gallery erupted. Saints shouted. Kings demanded their excuses back. The prosecutor tore the scroll, but each fragment continued to speak.

After the crack appeared in the light, voices entered faster than the scribes could record them. They did not accuse only Heaven. They accused kings, parents, teachers, lovers, armies, churches, markets, and themselves. Satan watched the Court lose its favorite geometry: good above, evil below, humanity in between. The record became a web instead. The empty chair behind the bench was not an accusation that Heaven alone was guilty. It was a demand that no power remain outside examination. That, Satan knew, was why the chair frightened them.

The nameless woman approached Satan as the court dissolved into argument. “Will they punish you?” “They already did.” “For what?” He looked toward the high sun, beautiful and cold. “For asking whether worship given under threat could be called love.” The woman considered this. “What happens to us now?” “You will be judged.” “By Hell?” “Hell judges no soul.

It carries out the sentence that truth makes necessary.” Behind the bench, a crack appeared in the light. Through it came the voices of the dead, no longer willing to let demons take credit for what human beings had done. Satan offered the woman his arm. “Come,” he said.

“The difficult testimony is about to begin.” And for the first time since accusation was invented, the empty chair belonged to Heaven.
